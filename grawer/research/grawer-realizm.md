## 1. Factual summary (with sources)

### 1.1 Techniques and how each one looks

| Technique | How the line is made | What it looks like in a photo |
|---|---|---|
| **Hand burin / graver** | A steel graver is pushed through the metal and lifts out a curl. The groove is V-shaped. It starts shallow, gets wider and deeper, then gets narrower and shallower as the stroke ends. | The line **swells and tapers**, and the ends come to points. A polished graver leaves mirror-like walls ("bright-cut"), so the cut flashes bright or dark depending on the angle. Look closely and you see small, irregular tool marks. Slips (the tool skidding out of the groove) leave short stray scratches. |
| **Pantograph / machine engraving** | A stylus traces a template, such as Gorton "Master Copy Type", and a cutter copies the movement at a smaller scale. The letters are **single-line (centreline) fonts**: one path per stroke. The stroke width equals the cutter-tip width. | The stroke is **monoline**: the same width everywhere, with the same rounded end on every stroke. It is usually a bit shallower than hand engraving. The traced copy can carry a small wobble from the operator's hand. Most mass-produced plated trays and presentation trays were done this way. |
| **Diamond-drag (scratch) engraving** | A non-rotating diamond tip is dragged across the surface. | The line is a **bright, hairline scratch**, the same width and depth along its whole length. On plain metal it gives contrast without any infill. Trade sources say it "compares to hand engraving" for fine lettering. |
| **Acid etching** | Acid eats the metal through a resist. | The lines are soft-edged and slightly "spidery", with **blunt starts and stops** and no tapering. They may be slightly undercut, with a flat-ish bottom. |

What this means for our tray: the existing pattern is made of very fine lines, all about the same width. It has rows of tiny circles and lines that break up here and there. That fits a mass-produced, machine-made decoration (a pantograph or diamond-scratch type, or possibly a fine etch) better than deep hand bright-cut. An inscription that matches it should be a **single-pass hairline cut along each letter's centreline**, the same width all along (a Gorton-style single-line letter). Its imperfections should come from hand-tracing wobble, skips and decades of polishing, not from a burin's swelling strokes.

### 1.2 How grooves catch light
- A V-cut behaves like two tiny mirrors. Which wall looks bright depends on where the light is, the angle of the surface and where the viewer stands (the angle the light comes in at equals the angle it leaves at). Under directional light one wall is bright and the other is dark, and the two swap when the piece moves.
- Under **diffuse, overcast-type light**, which is what our photo has, a fine scratch-cut breaks up the smooth reflection. It reads as a **thin, pale, frosted or whitish line that is slightly lighter than the satin-grey surface**. A tiny glint may show here and there. It does **not** show a bright edge next to a shadow edge. That is exactly how the floral pattern looks in `wzor-grawerunku-zblizenie.png`.
- Wider-angle or deeper cuts look darker. Trays with tarnish in the grooves show darker lines. Our tray's pattern reads light rather than dark, so its grooves are clean and shallow and polishing has kept them bright.

### 1.3 Ageing and imperfections
- **Polishing over decades** wears high and exposed areas first. On plated ware the plating thins and the base metal can show grey-yellow. Shallow engraved lines get softer and lose contrast, and some parts **fade almost completely** while other parts stay crisp.
- **Tarnish** collects in recesses, so on unpolished pieces the engraved lines darken. A well-polished piece reads bright and even.
- **Hand or traced imperfections**: lines that are not quite parallel, slightly uneven spacing, small wobbles in curves, slips and overshoots at the ends of strokes, re-cuts where a stroke was gone over twice (a second hairline next to the first), and skipped bits where the cutter rode up out of the groove.
- **Later use**: fine scratches from cutlery and cloths cross both the letters and the pattern in random directions, along with fingerprints and haze.

## 3. Prompt fragment bank (positive, physical, photographic)

Wording rule: never say "outline", "outlined", "contour", "embossed", "font" or "glitter", not even with a "no" in front, because naming them tends to bring them in. Describe each letter stroke as **one line**.

### 3.1 Line construction
- "each letter is written with a single continuous hairline scratch that runs along the centre of the stroke"
- "one fine cut line per stroke, as thin as the thinnest lines of the floral engraving"
- "a single-line pantograph lettering style: every stroke is the same hairline width, with softly rounded ends"
- "the letters are drawn by line alone, so the metal between and inside the strokes is the same untouched tray surface"
- "where two strokes meet, the hairlines simply cross or touch, like pen lines"

### 3.2 Tone under diffuse light
- "the cut lines read as pale, frosted, slightly whitish hairlines, just a shade lighter than the satin-grey metal around them"
- "low-contrast, tone-on-tone silver-on-silver lettering, exactly as faint and pale as the existing floral scrolls"
- "under the soft overcast daylight the grooves show no shadow side, only a fine light-scattering line with a few tiny isolated glints"
- "the lettering is as easy to see as the ornament next to it, and no more prominent"

### 3.3 Depth and surface continuity
- "a very shallow incised scratch, flush with the surface, a fraction of a tenth of a millimetre deep"
- "the tray's reflections, haze, smudges and fingerprints run continuously across the letters"
- "fine random cutlery and polishing scratches cross through some letters"

### 3.4 Irregularity: tracing hand and tool
- "slightly hand-traced: the baseline drifts gently, the letter spacing is a touch uneven, the round letters are a little egg-shaped"
- "a faint tremor in the long curves of C, e and a"
- "a few strokes overshoot their end by a millimetre, and one stroke has a short stray slip-mark beside it"
- "the line pressure varies, so some strokes are crisp and bright while others are faint and broken into short dashes where the cutter skipped"
- "one or two strokes show a second hairline running almost on top of the first, where the cut was gone over again"

### 3.5 Age and wear
- "decades of polishing have softened the inscription unevenly: parts of some letters have almost vanished, for example the bottom of the C and the tail of the a"
- "the inscription has the same age and wear as the floral pattern, so both look engraved at the same time"
- "a trace of grey tarnish sits in a few of the hairlines, while the rest are polished bright"

### 3.6 Photographic integration
- "rendered with the same slight phone-camera softness, JPEG grain and focus falloff as the surrounding pattern, never sharper than the ornament"
- "foreshortened in the exact perspective of the tray floor"
- "modest size: 'A Certain Era' spans about a quarter to a third of the inner oval's width"
- "the existing floral engraving stays exactly as it is in the original photo, line for line"

### 3.7 Glyph reminders
- "'VINTAGE' in small, widely spaced single-line capitals; its A is an open Λ, two strokes meeting at the top with nothing across the middle"
- "'Certain' and 'Era' use a single-storey a written as a circle with a short vertical stem"

### 3.8 Ready-to-paste core paragraph
> The inscription is cut the same way as the tray's existing floral pattern: every letter is written with one single continuous hairline scratch along the centre of the stroke, as thin as the thinnest lines of the ornament. Under the soft overcast daylight each hairline reads as a pale, frosted line only a shade lighter than the satin-grey metal, with a few tiny isolated glints, tone-on-tone and as faint as the floral scrolls beside it. The lettering is slightly hand-traced: the baseline drifts gently, round letters are a little uneven, line pressure varies so some strokes are crisp while others fade or break into short dashes, a couple of strokes overshoot or carry a tiny stray slip-mark, and decades of polishing have worn parts of some letters almost away. The tray's haze, smudges and fine random scratches run straight across the letters, and the inscription has the same soft phone-camera focus and grain as the rest of the photo.
